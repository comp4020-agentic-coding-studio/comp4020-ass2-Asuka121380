The first four weeks gave you a vocabulary for guitar sound that doesn't
depend on adjectives like "warm" or "bright". This assessment asks you to
actually use it: take a real recorded guitar sound and work out what is
physically happening in it, using evidence rather than impressions.

## The task

Choose (or, if one is provided, use) a short recording of an electric guitar.
Analyse it in terms of:

- **fundamental frequency** — what note is being played, and how does the
  pitch estimate move over the note's duration
- **harmonic spectrum** — which harmonics are present, and how their relative
  strength changes across the note
- **transient characteristics** — what the attack looks like, and how quickly
  and in what shape the sound decays
- **pickup position and likely guitar-electronics effects** — what the
  spectral balance suggests about where the signal was picked up and how it
  may have been shaped before it left the guitar
- **basic filtering** — any obvious high- or low-frequency emphasis or
  attenuation
- **evidence visible in the waveform or spectrum** — point to the specific
  feature of the plot that supports each claim you make

For every claim, say explicitly whether it is a **direct observation** (read
straight off the waveform or spectrum), a **scientifically supported
interpretation** (an observation plus a principle from Weeks 1-4), or an
**uncertain assumption** (a reasonable guess you can't fully support from the
signal alone).

## Important constraint

You are not expected to analyse distortion circuits or amplifier behaviour —
those haven't been taught yet at this point in the course. If the sample
shows obvious nonlinear distortion or amplifier coloration, it's fine to note
that it's present and out of scope for this analysis, rather than attempting
to explain its mechanism.

## Skills assessed

Observation; spectral reasoning; use of physical principles; clear technical
explanation.
