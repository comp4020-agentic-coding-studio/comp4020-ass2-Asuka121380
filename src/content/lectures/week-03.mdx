<WeekBand index={1} label="Where we left off" heading="A vibrating string is not yet a signal">

Week 2 explained where a string's vibration comes from and what shape it
takes: a sum of standing-wave modes, each with its own frequency and its own
spatial pattern of nodes and antinodes. But a vibrating string on its own
produces only mechanical motion. No electrical signal exists anywhere in the
instrument until something senses that motion — and what does the sensing,
and **where it sits**, turns out to already shape the tone before a single
cable is plugged in.

This week is the first of the course's two transductions: the point where the
signal stops being a moving object and starts being a voltage. Everything
from Week 4 onward operates on that voltage.

By the end of it you should be able to describe a magnetic pickup's basic
structure; explain, conceptually, how string motion induces a voltage; explain
why pickup position changes the measured harmonic balance; contrast neck and
bridge positions without relying only on subjective language; and describe, at
a broad level, why humbuckers use two coils.

</WeekBand>

<WeekBand index={2} label="Mechanism" heading="Structure, and what actually induces the voltage" layout="wide">

A magnetic pickup is built from a permanent magnet, one or more ferromagnetic
pole pieces, and a coil of wire wound around them — sitting beneath a string
that itself has to be ferromagnetic to interact with the field at all. The
magnet magnetises the part of the string directly above it, and the field of
that magnetised section threads back down through the coil. As the string
vibrates, the flux through the coil rises and falls, and a changing flux is
exactly what induces a voltage across a coil. It is the *change* that matters,
not the string's position by itself — which the figure below lets you test
directly.

<WeekBand index={3} label="Position" heading="A pickup samples the string at one point" layout="wide">

Week 2 showed that a string's vibration is a sum of modes, each with a
different spatial pattern. A pickup senses motion only at the one location it
sits at, so it effectively samples that sum at a single point — and because
each mode has a different amplitude at that point, position changes the
measured balance between harmonics, just as picking position did for
excitation in Week 2. The useful description is exactly that: *which*
partials are relatively stronger or weaker at that position, rather than
reaching for "warm" or "bright" on their own.

<WeekBand index={4} label="Try it" heading="Move the pickup, and listen" layout="wide" bleed>

Drag the pickup along the string and the sensitivity curve moves with it.
Press play and drag again: the sound moves too, because the filter processing
the recording is the same expression the curve plots — the nulls you can see
are the nulls you can hear. Watch the measured spectrum settle onto the
predicted curve while a note rings.

<WeekBand index={5} label="Two coils" heading="Single coil versus humbucker" layout="wide">

Everything so far has assumed one coil. A single-coil pickup is exactly
that — simple, and with nothing to reject the electromagnetic interference it
picks up from nearby equipment, which is the source of its characteristic hum.
A humbucker solves that with a second coil, and the solution is worth
understanding because the same trick appears again later in the chain.

<WeekChain week={3} />

Week 2 explained the modes a string supports. This week showed that pickup
placement samples those modes, converting motion at one specific point into a
voltage. But that voltage does not reach the rest of the signal chain
untouched — it immediately meets resistance, capacitance, cable, and the
guitar's own volume and tone controls. Week 4 studies that electrical system.
