## How does a magnetic pickup convert string motion into an electrical signal?

<p class="bench-prose">
Week 2 explained where a string's vibration comes from, but a vibrating
string on its own only produces mechanical motion — no electrical signal
exists until something senses that motion. That something is the pickup, and
what it senses, and where, turns out to already shape the resulting tone.
</p>

## Pickup structure and electromagnetic induction

<div class="bench-annotated">
  <PickupDiagram />
  <p class="bench-prose">
  A magnetic pickup is built from a permanent magnet, one or more
  ferromagnetic pole pieces, and a coil of wire wound around them — sitting
  beneath a string that itself needs to be ferromagnetic to interact with the
  field at all. As the string vibrates, its changing position changes the
  magnetic flux passing through the coil, and a changing flux is exactly what
  induces a voltage across that coil. It's the *change* in flux that matters,
  not the string's position by itself — a string held perfectly still,
  however close to the magnet, induces nothing.
  </p>
</div>

</BenchBand>

## Pickup position: sampling the string's modes

<p class="bench-prose">
Week 2 showed that a string's vibration is a sum of standing-wave modes, each
with its own spatial pattern of nodes and antinodes. A pickup only senses
motion at the one location it sits at, so it effectively samples that sum at
a single point — and because each mode has a different amplitude at that
point, pickup position changes the measured balance between harmonics, just
as picking position did for excitation in Week 2. A bridge-position pickup
sits closer to a region with relatively more high-frequency content; a
neck-position pickup sits somewhere with a different balance. The more useful
description is exactly that — *which* harmonics are relatively stronger or
weaker at that position — rather than reaching for language like "warm" or
"bright" on its own.
</p>

</BenchBand>

## Single-coil versus humbucker

<div class="bench-annotated">
  <CoilComparisonDiagram />
  <p class="bench-prose">
  A single-coil pickup is exactly one coil sensing the string directly — simple,
  but with nothing to reject electromagnetic interference picked up from
  nearby equipment, which is the source of its characteristic hum. A
  humbucker uses two coils, wound in opposite directions with opposite
  magnetic polarity: interference reaching both coils equally cancels out,
  while each coil's response to the string itself combines rather than
  cancelling. The detailed wiring that makes this work is beyond this week's
  scope — the broad principle is what matters here.
  </p>
</div>
