<WeekBand index={1} label="Where we left off" heading="Twelve weeks, one chain">

Every week since Week 1 has looked at one stage of the same chain in
isolation: a string's vibration, a pickup's sensitivity, a gain stage's
headroom, a clipping curve's shape, a filter's cutoff, a modulation effect's
sweep, a delay or reverb's decay, an amplifier's staged gain, a cabinet's
coloration. None of those stages is the whole story on its own. This week
introduces no new physics or signal processing — it asks a different
question of everything already covered: given a target sound, how do you
work backwards through this chain and choose settings, in order, that get
you there? By the end of this week you should be able to identify the major
variables that determine guitar tone, trace cause and effect across the
entire signal chain, design a guitar signal chain for a target outcome,
justify effect ordering using signal-processing principles, recognise
trade-offs between different engineering choices, and explain how multiple
different systems can produce perceptually similar outcomes.

<WeekBand index={2} label="The complete chain" heading="Thirteen links, one signal path" layout="wide">

Thirteen links, each one a week (or part of a week) of this course: a
player's action sets a string vibrating; the string's motion is sensed by a
pickup; the pickup's signal passes through the guitar's own electronics;
gain stages amplify it; nonlinear processing (clipping, fuzz) reshapes it;
filters carve its frequency content; modulation and delay/reverb add
time-based movement and repetition; an amplifier applies its own staged
gain, filtering, and nonlinearity; a speaker and cabinet convert the result
to acoustic pressure; that pressure travels through air; a listener
perceives it. Nothing here is new — this is Weeks 1 through 11, laid end to
end.

Restricting the picture to just the chain's linear stages — pickup tilt,
Tone/EQ, the amplifier's tone stack, the cabinet — shows how each one
layers onto the last, building up the chain's overall frequency response
one decision at a time:

<WeekBand index={3} label="Design method" heading="Reasoning backwards from a target description">

Start with a target description, not a list of settings. For example: *a
clear but heavily saturated lead tone with long sustain and enough midrange
emphasis to remain audible in a dense mix.*

Reasoning backwards from that description means making a decision at every
stage: which pickup position gives the brightness and sensitivity the tone
needs; whether EQ should shape the signal before or after distortion; how
much gain, and distributed across how many stages; which clipping
characteristic gives "heavily saturated" without losing "clear"; what
post-distortion filtering is needed to control harshness; whether
modulation adds movement or just clutter; whether delay or reverb helps
"long sustain" read as space rather than mud; what amplifier settings
reinforce the rest; and what cabinet response keeps the midrange audible.
Every one of those decisions was introduced somewhere in Weeks 1–11 — this
week is about making them together, deliberately, for a stated goal.

</WeekBand>

<WeekBand index={4} label="Order and trade-offs" heading="Why effects are not interchangeable">

Because most of this chain is a sequence of operations, and several of
those operations are nonlinear, *where* an effect sits changes what it
does — not just its own output, but what every stage after it receives. EQ
before distortion reshapes what the nonlinearity has to work with, changing
which harmonics get generated; EQ after distortion only reweights harmonics
the nonlinearity already created. The same logic applies to reverb before
versus after distortion, or modulation before versus after a gain stage.
There is no single universal "correct" pedal order — different orders are
different, deliberate design choices, each with its own consequence, the
way different clipping topologies or cabinet profiles were different,
deliberate choices earlier in the course.

<EffectOrderDiagram eqCutoffHz={2500} />

Every stage's settings trade one property against another, not just "more"
against "less": more saturation buys sustain and thickness at the cost of
note definition; extending the high end buys clarity and presence at the
cost of harshness; a heavier low end buys weight at the cost of mix
clarity; a longer reverb tail buys space and atmosphere at the cost of
note-to-note articulation. Designing a chain backwards from a target sound
means choosing where on each of these trade-offs the target actually sits —
not maximising every stage independently.

Engineering a guitar tone is not always about discovering the one correct
chain. Because several stages can each independently push the overall
sound brighter or darker, louder or quieter, two chains that make opposite
choices at individual stages can still converge on a broadly similar
overall shape. That doesn't mean the two chains sound identical in every
respect — only that "the right chain" is rarely unique.

This is the most integrated interaction in the course: pickup position,
Tone/EQ (and whether it sits before or after the gain stage), drive and
clipping shape, modulation, delay/reverb, amplifier drive and tone, and
cabinet profile, all wired into one chain in signal order. Change a control
early in the chain and listen for how everything downstream is affected —
including whether it still sounds like "the same guitar" through a
different amplifier and cabinet, or a genuinely different instrument. The
frequency-response plot below the controls tracks only the chain's linear
(filter) stages — distortion, modulation, and delay/reverb aren't single
fixed frequency responses, so they show up in what you hear, not in that
curve. This implementation is deliberately simplified: the goal is to
demonstrate how the stages interact, not to emulate real guitar equipment
precisely.

This course opened by asking how a vibrating string becomes the sound
recognised as electric-guitar tone, and has spent twelve weeks following
that question through every stage of the chain that answers it. The idea
to leave with: guitar tone is not produced by one instrument, pedal,
amplifier, or frequency curve. It emerges from a chain of interacting
physical and signal-processing systems.
