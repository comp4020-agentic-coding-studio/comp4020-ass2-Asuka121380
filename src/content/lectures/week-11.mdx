<WeekBand index={1} label="Where we left off" heading="The last physical stage before sound reaches the air">

Week 10 followed the signal to the amplifier's electrical output. That
output still isn't sound — it's a voltage across a speaker terminal. Turning
that voltage into acoustic pressure in the air is its own physical process,
with its own way of reshaping the signal, and it's the last stage in the
chain before a listener, or a microphone, hears anything at all. By the end
of this week you should be able to explain how a loudspeaker converts an
electrical signal into mechanical motion; explain why guitar speakers are
deliberately not flat, transparent transducers; describe resonance
conceptually as part of why a speaker's response isn't flat; compare
open-back and closed-back cabinets conceptually; explain, briefly, that
radiated sound depends on frequency and direction; explain that microphone
type, position, angle, and distance all affect a recorded tone; and connect
a cabinet's impulse response back to Week 9's delay/reverb ideas.

<WeekBand index={2} label="Transduction" heading="Electrical signal to mechanical motion" layout="wide">

A loudspeaker's voice coil sits inside a fixed magnetic field. Current from
the amplifier flows through the coil, and a current-carrying conductor in a
magnetic field experiences a force — so the coil, attached to the cone,
moves. Reverse the current's direction and the force reverses too. The
amplifier's electrical signal is therefore converted directly into the
cone's back-and-forth mechanical motion, and that motion is what pushes on
the surrounding air.

<WeekBand index={3} label="Coloration, not accident" heading="Why guitar speakers aren't flat">

A studio monitor is designed to reproduce a signal as transparently as
possible across the audible range. A guitar speaker is not designed that
way, and isn't trying to be: its cone, surround, and magnet structure have
their own mechanical resonances, and its frequency response rolls off and
peaks in ways a "flat" transducer deliberately avoids. That coloration
isn't a flaw to be corrected — for a guitar amplifier, it's a major,
expected part of the final tone.

<CabinetResponseDiagram />

A **bright** small speaker rolls off early at the low end but extends
further at the top; a **darker** cabinet rolls off the highs much sooner; a
**resonant** cabinet peaks near both ends instead of rolling off smoothly.
None of these is "correct" — they're different, deliberate colorations, the
same way different clipping topologies in Week 6 were different, deliberate
distortions rather than one being more accurate than another. A speaker
cone, its surround, and the air trapped inside a cabinet all have natural
mechanical or acoustic resonances — the same general idea Week 7 introduced
for electrical filters, just realised here mechanically and acoustically
instead of electrically. This course treats resonance here only
conceptually, not as detailed mechanical or room acoustics.

</WeekBand>

<WeekBand index={4} label="The enclosure" heading="Cabinet design, radiation, and the microphone">

The box a speaker is mounted in matters too. An **open-back** cabinet
leaves the rear of the cone exposed, so sound radiates from both the front
and the back; a **closed-back** cabinet seals the rear, so only the front
radiates. The cabinet's internal volume, and how many speakers share it,
also shape the result. This course doesn't require advanced enclosure-design
theory — only that the enclosure is a deliberate design choice, not
incidental packaging.

<CabinetDiagram />

How sound radiates from a cone also depends on frequency: lower frequencies
tend to radiate in most directions fairly evenly, while higher frequencies
become more directional, radiating more narrowly along the speaker's axis.
That's one reason a guitar cabinet can sound different depending on where,
relative to the speaker, a listener or a microphone stands. When a cabinet
is recorded rather than heard directly in the room, the microphone becomes
part of the signal path too: different microphone types respond
differently, and where a microphone is placed relative to the cone
(on-axis versus off-axis, near the center versus near the edge), the angle
it's aimed at, and the distance from the cabinet, all affect what's
actually captured. This course introduces that these factors matter
conceptually — it does not turn into a recording-engineering course, and
doesn't claim any one placement is universally "correct."

Week 9 introduced the impulse response as a way to describe a linear
system's behaviour: excite it with a single impulse, and its output over
time tells you everything about how it will treat any input. A recorded
speaker-cabinet-microphone combination has its own impulse response too —
it captures, in one linear description, the combined effect of the
speaker, the cabinet, the microphone, and its position, all at once.
That's exactly why a "cabinet impulse response" is a genuinely useful,
reusable recording: convolving a signal with it (Week 9's convolution
reverb, again) applies that whole combined coloration in one step.

</WeekBand>
