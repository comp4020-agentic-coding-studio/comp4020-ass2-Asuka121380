## Why can the same amplifier sound dramatically different through different speakers and cabinets?

<p class="bench-prose">
Week 10 followed the signal to the amplifier's electrical output. That output
still isn't sound — it's a voltage across a speaker terminal. Turning that
voltage into acoustic pressure in the air is its own physical process, with
its own way of reshaping the signal, and it's the last stage in the chain
before a listener (or a microphone) hears anything at all.
</p>

<div class="bench-callout bench-prose">

By the end of this week you should be able to: explain how a loudspeaker
converts an electrical signal into mechanical motion; explain why guitar
speakers are deliberately not flat, transparent transducers; describe
resonance conceptually as part of why a speaker's response isn't flat;
compare open-back and closed-back cabinets conceptually; explain, briefly,
that radiated sound depends on frequency and direction; explain that
microphone type, position, angle, and distance all affect a recorded tone;
and connect a cabinet's impulse response back to Week 9's delay/reverb ideas.

</div>

## Loudspeaker transduction: electrical signal to mechanical motion

<p class="bench-prose">
A loudspeaker's voice coil sits inside a fixed magnetic field. Current from
the amplifier flows through the coil, and a current-carrying conductor in a
magnetic field experiences a force — so the coil, attached to the cone,
moves. Reverse the current's direction and the force reverses too. The
amplifier's electrical signal is therefore converted directly into the
cone's back-and-forth mechanical motion, and that motion is what pushes on
the surrounding air.
</p>

## Why guitar speakers aren't flat

<p class="bench-prose">
A studio monitor is designed to reproduce a signal as transparently as
possible across the audible range. A guitar speaker is not designed that
way, and isn't trying to be: its cone, surround, and magnet structure have
their own mechanical resonances, and its frequency response rolls off and
peaks in ways a "flat" transducer deliberately avoids. That coloration isn't
a flaw to be corrected — for a guitar amplifier, it's a major, expected part
of the final tone.
</p>

<div class="bench-annotated">
  <CabinetResponseDiagram />
  <p class="bench-prose">
  A **bright** small speaker rolls off early at the low end but extends
  further at the top; a **darker** cabinet rolls off the highs much sooner;
  a **resonant** cabinet peaks near both ends instead of rolling off
  smoothly. None of these is "correct" — they're different, deliberate
  colorations, the same way different clipping topologies in Week 6 were
  different, deliberate distortions rather than one being more accurate
  than another.
  </p>
</div>

## Resonance

<p class="bench-prose">
A speaker cone, its surround, and the air trapped inside a cabinet all have
natural mechanical or acoustic resonances — frequencies at which they
respond more readily than others, the same general idea Week 7 introduced
for electrical filters, just realised here mechanically and acoustically
instead of electrically. A resonance shows up as a peak in the frequency
response, the same kind of peak a high-Q resonant filter produces. This
course treats resonance here only conceptually — not as detailed mechanical
or room acoustics.
</p>

## Cabinet design: open-back, closed-back, and what's inside

<p class="bench-prose">
The box a speaker is mounted in matters too. An **open-back** cabinet leaves
the rear of the cone exposed, so sound radiates from both the front and the
back; a **closed-back** cabinet seals the rear, so only the front radiates.
The cabinet's internal volume, and how many speakers share it, also shape
the result. This course doesn't require advanced enclosure-design theory —
only that the enclosure is a deliberate design choice, not incidental
packaging.
</p>

## Radiation

<p class="bench-prose">
How sound radiates from a cone also depends on frequency: lower frequencies
tend to radiate in most directions fairly evenly, while higher frequencies
become more directional, radiating more narrowly along the speaker's axis.
That's one reason a guitar cabinet can sound different depending on where,
relative to the speaker, a listener (or a microphone) stands — briefly
introduced here, not developed into a full acoustics treatment.
</p>

## Microphones: type, position, angle, distance

<p class="bench-prose">
When a cabinet is recorded rather than heard directly in the room, a
microphone becomes part of the signal path too. Different microphone types
respond differently; where a microphone is placed relative to the cone
(on-axis versus off-axis, near the center versus near the edge), the angle
it's aimed at, and the distance from the cabinet, all affect what's actually
captured. This course introduces that these factors matter conceptually — it
does not turn into a recording-engineering course, and doesn't claim any one
placement is universally "correct."
</p>

## Cabinet impulse responses

<p class="bench-prose">
Week 9 introduced the impulse response as a way to describe a linear
system's behaviour: excite it with a single impulse, and its output over
time tells you everything about how it will treat any input. A recorded
speaker-cabinet-microphone combination has its own impulse response too — it
captures, in one linear description, the combined effect of the speaker,
the cabinet, the microphone, and its position, all at once. That's exactly
why a "cabinet impulse response" is a genuinely useful, reusable recording:
convolving a signal with it (Week 9's convolution reverb, again) applies
that whole combined coloration in one step.
</p>
