<WeekBand index={1} label="No week before this one" heading="Tone as an engineering question">

"Tone" gets used loosely — warm, bright, aggressive, muddy. This course treats
it as an engineering question instead: two signals can share a pitch and a
loudness and still sound completely different, and that difference has a
name, a cause, and a measurable signature. This week sets up the vocabulary
and the signal chain that the rest of the course keeps returning to. By the
end of it you should be able to distinguish pitch, loudness, and timbre as
separate properties of a sound; recognise that different waveforms can share
a fundamental frequency; read a signal in both the time domain and the
frequency domain; identify the stages of the guitar signal chain; and explain
why tone is the product of a whole system, not any single component.

</WeekBand>

<WeekBand index={2} label="Vocabulary" heading="Pitch, loudness, and timbre">

Three separate properties get bundled together in casual listening.
**Pitch** is how high or low a note sounds, and corresponds to the
fundamental frequency of the vibration. **Loudness** is how intense the
sound is, corresponding to amplitude. **Timbre** is everything else — the
property that lets you tell a guitar apart from a violin playing the
identical pitch at the identical loudness. Timbre itself is shaped by
several contributors at once: the balance of harmonics in the spectrum,
transient behaviour at the start of a note, how the sound dies away,
modulation over time, nonlinear distortion, resonances in the instrument
and signal chain, and other temporal effects — no single number captures
it; it is the composite of all of these.

</WeekBand>

<WeekBand index={3} label="Two readings" heading="Time domain and frequency domain">

The same sound can be described two ways. A **time-domain** view — a
waveform — plots amplitude against time and shows how a signal literally
moves. A **frequency-domain** view — a spectrum — plots how much energy sits
at each frequency, and shows what that motion is built from. Neither view is
more "real" than the other; they're two readings of the same signal, and
this course moves between them constantly.

</WeekBand>

<WeekBand index={4} label="The system" heading="The guitar signal chain" layout="wide">

From the string's first vibration to the sound reaching a listener's ear, a
guitar's tone passes through every one of these stages — and every stage can
change it. That's why tone is a property of the whole system: the same
string and pickup can sound different behind a different set of
electronics, pedals, amplifier, speaker, or room, and each week of this
course studies one stage of this chain in more depth.
