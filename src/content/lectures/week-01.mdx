## What do we actually mean when we say that two guitars have different "tone"?

<p class="bench-prose">
"Tone" gets used loosely — warm, bright, aggressive, muddy. This course
treats it as an engineering question instead: two signals can share a pitch
and a loudness and still sound completely different, and that difference has
a name, a cause, and a measurable signature. This week sets up the vocabulary
and the signal chain that the rest of the course keeps returning to.
</p>

<div class="bench-callout bench-prose">

By the end of this week you should be able to: distinguish pitch, loudness,
and timbre as separate properties of a sound; recognise that different
waveforms can share a fundamental frequency; read a signal in both the time
domain and the frequency domain; identify the stages of the guitar signal
chain; and explain why tone is the product of a whole system, not any single
component.

</div>

## Pitch, loudness, and timbre

<p class="bench-prose">
Three separate properties get bundled together in casual listening. **Pitch**
is how high or low a note sounds, and corresponds to the fundamental
frequency of the vibration. **Loudness** is how intense the sound is,
corresponding to amplitude. **Timbre** is everything else — the property
that lets you tell a guitar apart from a violin playing the identical pitch
at the identical loudness.
</p>

<div class="bench-callout">

Timbre is shaped by several contributors at once: the balance of harmonics in
the spectrum, transient behaviour at the start of a note (attack), how the
sound dies away (decay), modulation over time, nonlinear distortion,
resonances in the instrument and signal chain, and other temporal effects.
No single number captures it — it is the composite of all of these.

</div>

## Time domain and frequency domain

<p class="bench-prose">
The same sound can be described two ways. A **time-domain** view — a
waveform — plots amplitude against time and shows how a signal literally
moves. A **frequency-domain** view — a spectrum — plots how much energy sits
at each frequency, and shows what that motion is built from. Neither view is
more "real" than the other; they're two readings of the same signal, and
this course moves between them constantly.
</p>
