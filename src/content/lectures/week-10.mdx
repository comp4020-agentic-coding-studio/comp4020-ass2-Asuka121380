## Why is a guitar amplifier more than a device that simply makes a signal louder?

<p class="bench-prose">
Weeks 5–9 covered gain, clipping, filtering, modulation, and delay/reverb as
things that happen to a signal *before* it reaches an amplifier. The
amplifier itself is not one simple multiplier — it is a small signal chain
of its own, and it can repeat several of those same processes: it applies
gain (more than once), it can filter, and it can distort. Understanding an
amplifier means recognising which of its stages is doing which of those
familiar jobs.
</p>

<div class="bench-callout bench-prose">

By the end of this week you should be able to: identify the major stages of
a simplified amplifier (preamp, tone stack, power amp); explain the
difference between a preamp stage and a power-amp stage; explain gain
staging — why distributing the same total amplification across multiple
stages changes the result; describe, conceptually, how tube and
solid-state amplifying elements differ without claiming one sounds
inherently better; explain negative feedback as a general technique, not a
guitar-specific trick; and explain why preamp gain and master level don't
do the same thing.

</div>

## The amplifier as another signal chain

<p class="bench-prose">
A simplified amplifier can be drawn the same way any other stage in this
course has been: input in, a sequence of blocks, output out. **Input → Preamp
→ Tone stack → Power amplifier → Speaker.** Some real amplifiers add further
stages (extra gain stages, phase-splitter or driver stages between preamp and
power amp) — this course doesn't require knowing every topology, only that
the amplifier is a chain with more than one place something can happen to
the signal.
</p>

<div class="bench-annotated">
  <SignalChainDiagram stages={amplifierStages} ariaLabel="Amplifier block diagram: Input, then Preamp (gain and asymmetric clipping), then Tone stack (reshapes frequency content), then Power amp (gain and softer saturation), then onward to the speaker, covered next week." />
  <p class="bench-prose">
  The **preamp** provides voltage gain, can filter, and can introduce
  nonlinear distortion — it's usually where a guitar amplifier's
  distortion character mostly comes from. The **power amplifier**'s main
  job is providing enough power to actually drive a speaker; it may also
  add its own, often gentler, nonlinear behaviour. The **tone stack** sits
  between them, reshaping which frequencies pass through, not how loud the
  signal is.
  </p>
</div>

## Gain staging: the same total gain, distributed differently

<p class="bench-prose">
Week 5 introduced gain staging as one stage's headroom. An amplifier with
more than one gain stage extends that idea: the same overall amount of
amplification can be placed mostly in the preamp, mostly in the power amp,
or split between them — and where it's placed changes which stage actually
reaches its own saturation point. This is a genuinely different-sounding
choice, not just a louder-or-quieter one.
</p>

<p class="bench-prose">
Both examples end at the same master-level setting, and both put a stage at
its saturation ceiling somewhere in the chain — but a different stage. That's
also why **master level** and **preamp gain** don't behave the same way:
master level sits after both nonlinear stages and only turns the already-
decided result up or down, while preamp gain decides whether, and how hard,
the preamp itself distorts.
</p>

## Two ways to build the amplifying element

<p class="bench-prose">
Historically, guitar amplifiers have been built around two different kinds
of amplifying component: **tubes** (valves), which amplify by controlling a
current through a vacuum using a charged grid, and **transistors**
(solid-state devices), which amplify by controlling current through a
semiconductor. Both are, at the level this course cares about, doing the
same job — turning a small input signal into a larger output signal — using
different physical mechanisms to do it.
</p>

<div class="bench-callout bench-prose">
It's tempting to reach for "tubes sound warm, transistors sound harsh" as a
settled fact. Treat that with caution: how any particular amplifying
element actually behaves depends heavily on the rest of its circuit (biasing,
feedback, gain staging, the tone stack, the power-amp design) and not on the
component type alone. This course doesn't take a side — it introduces both
conceptually and leaves sweeping "type X always sounds like Y" claims out.
</div>

## Negative feedback

<p class="bench-prose">
Many amplifier stages route a portion of their output back to their input
in a way that opposes the original signal — **negative feedback**. As a
general technique (not specific to guitar amplifiers, or even to audio), it
can trade away some raw gain in exchange for more predictable, more linear
behaviour, and it can change how a stage responds over time as well as in
level. Introduced here conceptually: this course doesn't work through the
feedback-network mathematics of any specific real circuit.
</p>

## Preamp distortion versus power-amp distortion

<p class="bench-prose">
Because the preamp and power amp are separate stages, each with its own
gain and its own nonlinearity, a signal can be distorted at one, the other,
both, or neither. A preamp typically clips a relatively small, low-power
signal, and — as with earlier weeks' clipping circuits — that clipping is
often asymmetric. A power amplifier is driving a much higher-power signal
into a speaker's load, and its own saturation, when it happens, is often
comparatively softer. These are two different nonlinear elements in the
same chain, not two names for one thing.
</p>

Weeks 5 through 9 covered every transformation this course teaches that
happens *before* amplification: gain, clipping, filtering, modulation,
delay and reverb. This week showed that the amplifier itself isn't a
separate kind of thing — it's a further, small signal chain that can repeat
