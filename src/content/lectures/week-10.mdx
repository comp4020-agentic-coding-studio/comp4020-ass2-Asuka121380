<WeekBand index={1} label="Where we left off" heading="The amplifier as a further signal chain">

Weeks 5 through 9 covered every transformation this course teaches that
happens *before* amplification: gain, clipping, filtering, modulation, and
delay/reverb. This week shows that the amplifier itself is not one simple
multiplier — it is a small signal chain of its own, and it can repeat
several of those same processes: it applies gain (more than once), it can
filter, and it can distort. By the end of this week you should be able to
identify the major stages of a simplified amplifier (preamp, tone stack,
power amp); explain the difference between a preamp stage and a power-amp
stage; explain gain staging; describe, conceptually, how tube and
solid-state amplifying elements differ without claiming one sounds
inherently better; explain negative feedback as a general technique; and
explain why preamp gain and master level don't do the same thing.

<SignalChainDiagram stages={amplifierStages} ariaLabel="Amplifier block diagram: Input, then Preamp (gain and asymmetric clipping), then Tone stack (reshapes frequency content), then Power amp (gain and softer saturation), then onward to the speaker, covered next week." />

A simplified amplifier can be drawn the same way any other stage in this
course has been: input in, a sequence of blocks, output out. Some real
amplifiers add further stages — extra gain stages, phase-splitter or driver
stages between preamp and power amp — but this course doesn't require
knowing every topology, only that the amplifier is a chain with more than
one place something can happen to the signal. The **preamp** provides
voltage gain, can filter, and can introduce nonlinear distortion — it's
usually where a guitar amplifier's distortion character mostly comes from.
The **power amplifier**'s main job is providing enough power to actually
drive a speaker; it may also add its own, often gentler, nonlinear
behaviour. The **tone stack** sits between them, reshaping which
frequencies pass through, not how loud the signal is.

</WeekBand>

<WeekBand index={3} label="Gain staging" heading="The same total gain, distributed differently">

Week 5 introduced gain staging as one stage's headroom. An amplifier with
more than one gain stage extends that idea: the same overall amount of
amplification can be placed mostly in the preamp, mostly in the power amp,
or split between them — and where it's placed changes which stage actually
reaches its own saturation point. This is a genuinely different-sounding
choice, not just a louder-or-quieter one.

Both examples end at the same master-level setting, and both put a stage at
its saturation ceiling somewhere in the chain — but a different stage.
That's also why **master level** and **preamp gain** don't behave the same
way: master level sits after both nonlinear stages and only turns the
already-decided result up or down, while preamp gain decides whether, and
how hard, the preamp itself distorts.

</WeekBand>

<WeekBand index={4} label="Two amplifying elements, two kinds of distortion" heading="Tubes vs. transistors, negative feedback, preamp vs. power-amp clipping">

Historically, guitar amplifiers have been built around two different kinds
of amplifying component: **tubes** (valves), which amplify by controlling a
current through a vacuum using a charged grid, and **transistors**
(solid-state devices), which amplify by controlling current through a
semiconductor. Both are, at the level this course cares about, doing the
same job — turning a small input signal into a larger output signal — using
different physical mechanisms to do it. It's tempting to reach for "tubes
sound warm, transistors sound harsh" as a settled fact. Treat that with
caution: how any particular amplifying element actually behaves depends
heavily on the rest of its circuit (biasing, feedback, gain staging, the
tone stack, the power-amp design) and not on the component type alone. This
course doesn't take a side.

Many amplifier stages route a portion of their output back to their input
in a way that opposes the original signal — **negative feedback**. As a
general technique (not specific to guitar amplifiers, or even to audio), it
can trade away some raw gain in exchange for more predictable, more linear
behaviour, and it can change how a stage responds over time as well as in
level.

Because the preamp and power amp are separate stages, each with its own
gain and its own nonlinearity, a signal can be distorted at one, the other,
both, or neither. A preamp typically clips a relatively small, low-power
signal, and — as with earlier weeks' clipping circuits — that clipping is
often asymmetric. A power amplifier is driving a much higher-power signal
into a speaker's load, and its own saturation, when it happens, is often
comparatively softer. These are two different nonlinear elements in the
same chain, not two names for one thing.

Weeks 5 through 9 covered every transformation this course teaches that
happens *before* amplification: gain, clipping, filtering, modulation,
delay and reverb. This week showed that the amplifier itself isn't a
separate kind of thing — it's a further, small signal chain that can repeat
several of those same processes (gain, more than once; filtering;
nonlinear distortion) in its own arrangement. But an amplifier's electrical
output still isn't sound in the air. Week 11 follows the signal through
its last physical stage: the loudspeaker and cabinet that turn this
electrical signal back into acoustic sound.
