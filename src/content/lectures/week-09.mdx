<WeekBand index={1} label="Where we left off" heading="From one delayed copy to a feedback loop">

Chorus, flanger, and phaser all combined a signal with one modulated copy of
itself. This week asks what happens once that delayed copy is fed back into
its own delay line, round and round, rather than summed just once. By the end
of this week you should be able to explain a delay line and its feedback
loop; explain why decaying repeats — not runaway repeats — are the normal
case; connect feedback delay to the comb filtering met in Week 8; describe
the structure of a natural reverb (direct sound, early reflections, dense
decaying tail); and distinguish algorithmic from convolution reverb.

<WeekBand index={2} label="Feedback delay" heading="One loop, recirculated" layout="wide">

<FeedbackLoopDiagram />

A **delay line** stores a signal and releases it again some fixed time
later. On its own that produces one repeat. Feeding a fraction `g` of that
delayed output back into the delay's own input, alongside the new incoming
signal, produces a whole series of repeats: the first echo re-enters the
delay and comes back out again, smaller, over and over. Because `g` is
always kept below one, each pass round the loop is quieter than the last —
the series decays rather than growing without bound. A **wet/dry mix**
control then blends that whole decaying series back in with the original,
undelayed signal.

<FeedbackCombDiagram />

Feeding a delayed copy back into itself is exactly the mechanism behind
Week 8's flanger comb filter, just recirculated instead of summed once.
The same regularly spaced peaks and notches appear at the same fixed
spacing (set by the delay time), but recirculating the signal through the
loop repeatedly sharpens them: more feedback produces taller peaks and
deeper notches at that same spacing, without moving where they sit.

</WeekBand>

<WeekBand index={3} label="From echoes to rooms" heading="What a reverberant space actually contains">

A single feedback delay produces a sparse, evenly spaced set of repeats —
recognisable as discrete echoes rather than a room. Natural reverberation is
what happens once a sound reflects off *every* surface in an enclosed space,
producing far more reflections than one delay line can, arriving from every
direction at once. That structure has three parts in sequence: the **direct
sound** (arriving first, straight from source to listener), the **early
reflections** (a handful of distinct, separately audible bounces off nearby
surfaces), and a **dense reverberant tail** (thousands of reflections
arriving so closely together they blur into one continuous decaying wash).
Two properties describe that tail: **room size**, which sets how far apart
the early reflections are spaced, and **decay time**, which sets how long
the tail takes to die away.

Two families of reverb reproduce that structure differently.
**Algorithmic reverb** never records a real space at all — it builds the
direct/early/tail structure out of networks of delay lines and feedback
loops (an extension of exactly the feedback loop above), tuned to produce a
plausible early-reflection pattern and a smoothly decaying tail.
**Convolution reverb** instead measures a real room's actual impulse
response — its reflections, recorded directly — and applies that measured
response to any input signal, reproducing that specific space's reflections
rather than an approximation of one.

</WeekBand>
