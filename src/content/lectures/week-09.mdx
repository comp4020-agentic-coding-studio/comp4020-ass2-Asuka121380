## How can delayed copies of a signal create the perception of echo, distance, and acoustic space?

<p class="bench-prose">
Week 8 used a very short, modulated delay purely as an interference effect —
chorus and flanging never let the delayed copy repeat. This week removes
that restriction: instead of a single delayed copy, the delay's own output
is fed back into itself, producing a whole decaying series of repeats. The
same idea, scaled up and made irregular rather than periodic, is also the
basic mechanism behind simulating reverberant space.
</p>

## The delay line, and what feedback adds to it

<p class="bench-prose">
A **delay line** simply outputs whatever it received some fixed time ago. On
its own, mixing a delay's output back with the dry signal (wet/dry mix)
gives a single, one-time repeat — useful, but not yet an echo you can hear
trailing off. **Feedback** changes that: a fraction of the delay's own
output, always less than one, is returned to feed the delay's input again.
Each time round the loop the signal is quieter than the time before, so the
result is a whole series of repeats that gets progressively softer — an
echo, rather than one repeat or an ever-growing runaway signal.
</p>

<div class="bench-annotated">
  <FeedbackLoopDiagram />
  <p class="bench-prose">
  Everything about how many audible repeats there are, and how quickly they
  fade, comes from this one loop: a longer delay time spaces the repeats
  further apart in time; a feedback gain closer to one keeps each repeat
  louder relative to the last, so the echo trails off more slowly.
  </p>
</div>

## Comb filtering, revisited

<p class="bench-prose">
Week 8 showed that summing a dry signal with *one* delayed copy produces a
comb filter — evenly spaced peaks and notches in the frequency response.
Feeding that delayed copy back into the delay, rather than summing it just
once, keeps recirculating it: the same peak and notch spacing survives (it's
still set entirely by the delay time), but more feedback means more energy
recirculates before it decays, which sharpens the same pattern into taller
peaks and deeper notches at a *fixed* delay time — no sweeping LFO required,
just how much feedback there is.
</p>

  differs. Compare this to Week 8's flanger response — the mechanism (dry
  plus delayed copy, interfering) is the same, it's just recirculated here
  instead of summed once.
  </p>
</div>

## Natural reverb: not one echo, but thousands

<p class="bench-prose">
A real acoustic space doesn't return one clean echo — it returns an
enormous number of them, arriving from every surface a sound bounces off.
That series has a recognisable structure in three parts: the **direct
sound** arriving first with no delay; a handful of **early reflections**
— the first, individually distinguishable bounces off nearby surfaces; and
then a **dense, decaying tail** as the reflections multiply faster than
they can be told apart and blur into a continuous, fading wash. **Room
size** governs how far apart the early reflections and the whole tail are
spread in time — a bigger room means a longer gap before anything comes
back; **decay** governs how quickly the tail's energy dies away, largely
set by how absorptive the room's surfaces are.
</p>

## Building reverb without measuring a real room

<p class="bench-prose">
**Algorithmic reverb** builds this same direct/early/dense-tail shape out of
electronic building blocks rather than a physical space: conceptually, a
small network of feedback delays of different, unrelated lengths, summed
together. Because the lengths are unrelated to one another, their repeats
don't line up into an audible periodic pattern the way a single feedback
delay's do — the combined result sounds like a dense, continuous decay
instead of a comb-filtered echo. This is a deliberate engineering
approximation of a room's behaviour, not a claim about any specific real
algorithm or product.
</p>

<p class="bench-prose">
A different approach, **convolution reverb**, starts from an **impulse
response** — a recording of how a specific real (or synthetic) space
responds to a single, instantaneous test click. That response already
contains the space's own direct sound, early reflections, and decay tail,
captured directly rather than approximated. Playing a new signal through
that space's convolution reverb means combining every sample of the new
signal with the entire impulse response, at every point in time — a
much heavier calculation than a handful of feedback delays, which is why
this is presented here conceptually rather than worked through
mathematically.
</p>

## Try it: echo and reverb, from the same feedback-delay idea

<p class="bench-prose">
Switch between Echo and Reverb. Time/Feedback/Mix always control the same
underlying thing — how long the loop is, how much comes back round it, and
how much wet signal is mixed in — but in Echo mode that's one delay line and
a single feedback loop, while in Reverb mode it's several such loops at
