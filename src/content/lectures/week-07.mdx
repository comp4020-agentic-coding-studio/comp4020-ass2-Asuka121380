## Frequency response, cutoff, and the filter family

<p class="bench-prose">
A filter's **frequency response** is simply its gain plotted as a function
of frequency: how much of each frequency component survives. A **low-pass**
filter passes frequencies below some **cutoff frequency** and reduces those
above it — Week 4's passive tone control was already one example. A
**high-pass** filter is its mirror image, passing what's above cutoff and
reducing what's below. A **band-pass** filter passes a range in between and
reduces frequencies on both sides of it; a **band-reject** filter does the
opposite, reducing a range in the middle while passing what's above and
below. Introduced at this level, band-pass and band-reject are exactly a
low-pass and a high-pass response combined, rather than a separate
mechanism to learn from scratch.
</p>

<div class="bench-annotated">
  <SignalChainDiagram
    stages={[
      { label: "Input", caption: "many frequencies present" },
      { label: "Filter", caption: "frequency-dependent gain" },
      { label: "Output", caption: "some frequencies emphasised, others reduced" },

  <p class="bench-prose">
  Whatever the filter's shape, the underlying operation is the same: a
  gain that depends on frequency, applied to every frequency component of
  the input at once.
  </p>
</div>

### Resonance and Q

<p class="bench-prose">
Some filters don't just roll off past cutoff — they briefly boost
frequencies right around it before rolling off, a **resonant peak**. **Q**
controls how narrow and tall that peak is: a low Q gives a broad, gentle
emphasis (or none at all), while a high Q gives a sharp, narrow spike right
at the cutoff frequency. A plain low-pass or high-pass filter can be built
with no resonance at all — Q only becomes an audible, adjustable parameter
once a filter is deliberately designed to have one.
</p>

## Passive versus active filters, and tone stacks

<p class="bench-prose">
Week 4's tone control was a **passive** filter: built only from resistors
and capacitors, with no power supply and no amplification, at the cost of
inevitably reducing the signal somewhat even at frequencies it's meant to
pass. An **active** filter adds an amplifying stage (typically built around
an operational amplifier) to the same resistor-capacitor idea, which can
compensate for that loss or even boost a chosen range instead of only
reducing others — the broad distinction that matters here, without going
into the amplifier circuitry itself.
</p>

<p class="bench-prose">
A **tone stack** — the bass/middle/treble network found in many guitar
amplifiers — is several of these frequency-shaping ideas wired together so
they interact. That interaction is the important, easily-missed point: the
three controls are not three independent filters quietly agreeing to share
a signal. Adjusting one can measurably shift what the others appear to do,
because the same resistor and capacitor networks are shared between them.
Reproducing any specific tone stack's exact interacting response accurately
would need specific circuit values this course doesn't have grounds to
assert — the qualitative lesson (shared components mean interacting
controls) is what this week is scoped to.
</p>

## EQ before distortion versus EQ after it

<p class="bench-prose">
Week 5 and 6 showed that a nonlinear gain stage generates new harmonic
content from whatever reaches it. That makes EQ placement around a
distortion stage a genuinely different decision each way, not just a matter
of taste — a guitar-specific application of everything above.
</p>

<div class="bench-annotated">
  <SignalChainDiagram stages={eqBeforeStages} ariaLabel="EQ placed before the gain and clipping stage." />
  <p class="bench-prose">
  **EQ before distortion** changes which frequencies drive the nonlinear
  stage in the first place — cutting bass before clipping, for instance,
  changes what gets clipped and so changes which harmonics the clipping
  stage generates.
  </p>
</div>

<div class="bench-annotated">
  <SignalChainDiagram stages={eqAfterStages} ariaLabel="EQ placed after the gain and clipping stage." />
  <p class="bench-prose">
  **EQ after distortion** instead shapes a spectrum that already contains
  whatever harmonics clipping just created — it can remove or emphasise
  those new frequencies, but it can no longer change which ones were
  generated to begin with.
  </p>
</div>
