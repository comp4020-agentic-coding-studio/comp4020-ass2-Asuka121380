## Why does clipping sound different from circuit to circuit?

Week 5 showed that clipping a sine wave adds harmonic content that wasn't
there before. But two circuits that both "clip the signal" don't necessarily
clip it the same way — and the shape of the clip determines which new
harmonics appear.

By the end of this week you should be able to:

- identify the main stages of a simplified distortion pedal;
- explain how a gain stage and a clipping stage divide the work;
- distinguish symmetric from asymmetric clipping, and connect the difference
  to a change in harmonic content;
- compare clipping applied inside a feedback loop with clipping applied
  directly to the signal, conceptually;
- explain why overdrive, distortion and fuzz are better understood as
  different *kinds* of nonlinearity than as different *amounts* of the same
  effect.

## Inside a simplified pedal

A distortion pedal can be reduced to four stages:

```text
Input
  ↓
Gain stage
  ↓
Clipping stage
  ↓
Tone filter
  ↓
Output
```

The **gain stage** (built from an op-amp, a practical building block for
amplification — its internal transistor detail isn't needed here) raises the
signal past the level the next stage can pass without limiting it. The more
gain applied here, the harder the following stage clips.

The **clipping stage** is where Week 5's abstract "clipping function" becomes
a real circuit. **Diodes** are the usual nonlinear element: a diode conducts
more readily in one direction than the other, and arranged across a signal
path, that behaviour imposes a soft limit on the waveform. That's a
conceptual model of what a diode does to this signal, not a full account of
semiconductor physics.

### Symmetric versus asymmetric clipping

If the positive and negative halves of the waveform are limited the same
way, the clipping is **symmetric** — the output stays close to odd-symmetric,
which mostly reinforces odd harmonics already present in the input.

If the two halves are limited *differently*, the clipping is **asymmetric**
— breaking that symmetry introduces harmonics that a symmetric curve
wouldn't produce, changing the harmonic *balance*, not just the harmonic
*count*.

### Where the clipping element sits

The same diode idea can appear in different places in a circuit:

- **clipping to ground** — the nonlinear element sits directly across the
  signal, limiting its swing once it reaches a threshold;
- **feedback-loop clipping** — the nonlinear element sits inside an
  amplifier's feedback path instead, which shapes the transfer curve
  differently even though the underlying diode behaviour is the same.

Two different circuit contexts for the same component produce two different
input-output curves — which is one reason two "distortion" circuits built
from similar parts can still sound different.

### Fuzz is not just "more distortion"

Fuzz circuits often push further than a single diode pair: additional gain
stages, transistor behaviour, and stronger waveshaping than simple diode
clipping. There isn't one fuzz topology — it's a family of stronger, often
less predictable, nonlinear transformations, not a single extra notch on a
"how distorted" dial.

Any claim more specific than this — about a particular named pedal, a
specific transistor type, or a historical circuit's exact component values —
needs a real source, and none is asserted here.

## Connection to Week 5, and where this goes next

Week 5 introduced clipping as a mathematical transformation: an input-output
curve that stops being a straight line once the signal exceeds a system's
headroom. This week implemented that transformation with real circuit
elements — gain stages, diodes, and the different contexts a nonlinear
element can sit in.

Clipping's real effect, from the last section, is to generate additional
harmonic content that wasn't in the original signal. The next step in the
signal chain is to ask what happens to that new, wider spectrum once it
reaches a filter — which selectively keeps, removes, or emphasises parts of
it rather than passing all of it through unchanged.
