<WeekBand index={1} label="Where we left off" heading="From a mathematical curve to a real circuit">

Week 5 showed that clipping a sine wave adds harmonic content that wasn't
there before, using clipping as an abstract mathematical transformation. But
two circuits that both "clip the signal" don't necessarily clip it the same
way — and the shape of the clip decides which new harmonics appear. By the
end of this week you should be able to identify the stages of a simplified
distortion pedal; explain how a gain stage and a clipping stage divide the
work; distinguish symmetric from asymmetric clipping by their effect on
harmonic content; compare clipping-to-ground with feedback-loop clipping
conceptually; and explain why overdrive, distortion and fuzz are different
*kinds* of nonlinearity rather than different *amounts* of one effect.

<WeekBand index={2} label="Inside a simplified pedal" heading="Gain stage, then clipping stage" layout="wide">

<SignalChainDiagram />

The **gain stage** raises the signal past the level the next stage can pass
without limiting it — the more gain applied here, the harder the following
stage clips. The **clipping stage** is where Week 5's abstract "clipping
function" becomes a real circuit, usually built from diodes: a diode
conducts more readily in one direction than the other, arranged across a
signal path that imposes a soft limit on the waveform. This is a conceptual
model of what a diode does here, not a full account of semiconductor
physics.

<GainStageDiagram />

Pushed far enough, the waveform's peaks start to flatten before they ever
reach the clipping stage's threshold — headroom is what "far enough" means.

</WeekBand>

<WeekBand index={4} label="Circuit context" heading="Where the clipping element sits">

<ClippingPositionDiagram />

Two different circuit contexts for the same diode behaviour produce two
different input-output curves — one reason two "distortion" circuits built
from similar parts can still sound different. Fuzz circuits usually push
further than a single diode pair — extra gain stages and stronger
waveshaping than simple diode clipping — but there isn't one fuzz topology to
name: it's a family of stronger, often less predictable transformations, not
a fixed extra notch on a "how distorted" dial. Anything more specific — a
named pedal, a transistor type, a historical circuit's component values —
would need a real source, and none is asserted here.

</WeekBand>

<WeekBand index={7} label="The chain" heading="Where this sits, and what comes next" layout="wide">

<WeekChain week={6} />

Week 5 introduced clipping as a mathematical transformation — an
input-output curve that stops being a straight line once a signal exceeds a
system's headroom. This week implemented that transformation with real
circuit elements: gain stages, diodes, and the different contexts a
nonlinear element can sit in. Clipping's real effect is to generate
additional harmonic content that wasn't in the original signal. Next week
asks what happens to that new, wider spectrum once it reaches a filter —
which selectively keeps, removes, or emphasises parts of it, rather than
passing all of it through unchanged.
