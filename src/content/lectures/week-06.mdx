## Why does clipping sound different from circuit to circuit?

<p class="bench-prose">
Week 5 showed that clipping a sine wave adds harmonic content that wasn't
there before. But two circuits that both "clip the signal" don't necessarily
clip it the same way — and the shape of the clip decides which new harmonics
appear.
</p>

<div class="bench-callout bench-prose">

By the end of this week you should be able to: identify the stages of a
simplified distortion pedal; explain how a gain stage and a clipping stage
divide the work; distinguish symmetric from asymmetric clipping by their
effect on harmonic content; compare clipping-to-ground with feedback-loop
clipping conceptually; and explain why overdrive, distortion and fuzz are
different *kinds* of nonlinearity rather than different *amounts* of one
effect.

</div>

## Inside a simplified pedal

<div class="bench-annotated">
  <SignalChainDiagram />
  <p class="bench-prose">
  The <strong>gain stage</strong> raises the signal past the level the next
  stage can pass without limiting it — the more gain applied here, the harder
  the following stage clips. The <strong>clipping stage</strong> is where
  Week 5's abstract "clipping function" becomes a real circuit, usually built
  from diodes: a diode conducts more readily in one direction than the other,
  arranged across a signal path that imposes a soft limit on the waveform.
  This is a conceptual model of what a diode does here, not a full account of
  semiconductor physics.
  </p>
</div>

## Where the clipping element sits

<div class="bench-annotated">
  <ClippingPositionDiagram />
  <p class="bench-prose">
  Two different circuit contexts for the same diode behaviour produce two
  different input-output curves — one reason two "distortion" circuits built
  from similar parts can still sound different.
  </p>
</div>

<p class="bench-scope-note">
Fuzz circuits usually push further than a single diode pair — extra gain
stages and stronger waveshaping than simple diode clipping — but there isn't
one fuzz topology to name: it's a family of stronger, often less predictable
transformations, not a fixed extra notch on a "how distorted" dial. Anything
more specific — a named pedal, a transistor type, a historical circuit's
component values — would need a real source, and none is asserted here.
</p>
