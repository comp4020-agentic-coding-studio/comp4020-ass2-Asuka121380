## Why can passive components inside a guitar significantly change its tone?

<p class="bench-prose">
Week 3 produced a voltage at the pickup. That voltage doesn't reach the
amplifier untouched — inside the guitar, and along the cable to the amp, it
passes through resistors, potentiometers, and capacitors that are entirely
passive (no power supply, no amplification) yet still measurably reshape the
signal's frequency content before anything resembling a pedal is involved.
</p>

<div class="bench-callout bench-prose">

high-frequency response; and read a simple frequency-response curve.

## Inside a simplified passive circuit

<div class="bench-annotated">
  <SignalChainDiagram stages={circuitStages} />
  <p class="bench-prose">
  The pickup behaves like a voltage source feeding a chain of passive parts.
  A **potentiometer** is a variable resistor: as a **volume control**, it's
  wired to send a variable fraction of the signal to ground instead of
  onward, turning the pot down sends proportionally more away. The
  **tone control** uses a potentiometer differently — as a variable
  resistance in series with a fixed **capacitor** to ground. A capacitor's
  impedance falls as frequency rises, so more of the signal's high-frequency
  content gets diverted to ground as that resistance changes.
  </p>
</div>

### The tone control as an RC low-pass

<div class="bench-annotated">
  <PotentiometerDiagram />
  <p class="bench-prose">
  Together, the tone pot's resistance and the fixed capacitor form a
  **low-pass filter**, with cutoff frequency f_c = 1/(2πRC): a larger
  resistance or a larger capacitance both lower the cutoff, removing more of
  the high end. This is exactly why a passive tone control isn't simply a
  volume control for treble — it selectively removes high-frequency energy
  above the cutoff rather than turning everything down equally.
  </p>
</div>

## Pickup and cable interaction, and buffers

<p class="bench-prose">
A pickup isn't an ideal voltage source — its own construction gives it a
non-negligible source impedance, and the instrument cable connecting it to
the amplifier adds its own capacitance in the process. That cable
capacitance, combined with the guitar's and amp's input impedance, forms
another low-pass effect: a longer or higher-capacitance cable can measurably
soften the signal's high end before the amplifier ever sees it. A **buffer**
— briefly, an active stage placed early in the signal path — reduces this
interaction between a high-impedance source and whatever comes after it,
without needing this week's circuit analysis to go any deeper.
</p>

</BenchBand>
