<WeekBand index={1} label="Where we left off" heading="From a voltage to a filtered signal">

Week 3 followed the string's motion into a voltage at the pickup. That
voltage doesn't reach the amplifier untouched — inside the guitar, and along
the cable to the amp, it passes through resistors, potentiometers, and
capacitors that are entirely passive (no power supply, no amplification) yet
still measurably reshape the signal's frequency content before anything
resembling a pedal is involved. By the end of this week you should be able to
identify resistors, potentiometers, and capacitors in a simplified guitar
circuit; describe what volume and tone controls actually do; explain,
qualitatively, how resistance and capacitance combine into a filter; relate
cable capacitance to high-frequency response; and read a simple
frequency-response curve.

<WeekBand index={2} label="Inside the guitar" heading="A simplified passive circuit" layout="wide">

<SignalChainDiagram stages={circuitStages} />

The pickup behaves like a voltage source feeding a chain of passive parts. A
**potentiometer** is a variable resistor: as a **volume control**, it's wired
to send a variable fraction of the signal to ground instead of onward —
turning the pot down sends proportionally more away. The **tone control**
uses a potentiometer differently — as a variable resistance in series with a
fixed **capacitor** to ground. A capacitor's impedance falls as frequency
rises, so more of the signal's high-frequency content gets diverted to
ground as that resistance changes.

</WeekBand>

<WeekBand index={3} label="The filter" heading="The tone control as an RC low-pass" layout="wide">

<PotentiometerDiagram />

Together, the tone pot's resistance and the fixed capacitor form a
**low-pass filter**, with cutoff frequency f_c = 1/(2πRC): a larger
resistance or a larger capacitance both lower the cutoff, removing more of
the high end. This is exactly why a passive tone control isn't simply a
volume control for treble — it selectively removes high-frequency energy
above the cutoff rather than turning everything down equally.

</WeekBand>

<WeekBand index={4} label="Cable and buffers" heading="Pickup and cable interaction">

A pickup isn't an ideal voltage source — its own construction gives it a
non-negligible source impedance, and the instrument cable connecting it to
the amplifier adds its own capacitance in the process. That cable
capacitance, combined with the guitar's and amp's input impedance, forms
another low-pass effect: a longer or higher-capacitance cable can measurably
soften the signal's high end before the amplifier ever sees it. A **buffer**
— briefly, an active stage placed early in the signal path — reduces this
interaction between a high-impedance source and whatever comes after it,
without needing this week's circuit analysis to go any deeper.

</WeekBand>
