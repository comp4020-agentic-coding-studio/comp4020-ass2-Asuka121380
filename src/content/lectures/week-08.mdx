## How can continuously changing delay, phase, pitch, or amplitude create the sensation of movement?

<p class="bench-prose">
Every transformation so far — gain, clipping, filtering — has been static:
set a value and it stays put while a note rings out. This week asks what
happens once a parameter is deliberately made to change continuously over
time, on its own clock, independent of anything the player does. Five
familiar effects — tremolo, vibrato, chorus, flanger, and phaser — turn out
to share one mechanism underneath rather than being five unrelated pedals.
</p>

<div class="bench-callout bench-prose">

By the end of this week you should be able to: explain what an LFO is;
distinguish tremolo from vibrato; explain chorus as a short modulated
delayed copy mixed with the original; explain flanging as the same idea with
a much shorter delay; explain phasing conceptually, through phase-shifting
filter stages rather than delay; and connect interference — constructive and
destructive — to what any of this actually sounds like.

</div>

## The LFO: a control signal, not a sound

<p class="bench-prose">
A **low-frequency oscillator (LFO)** is a plain, slow oscillator — a sine
wave is the simplest case — that is never sent to the output. Instead its
value at each instant is used to steer some *other* parameter: a gain, a
delay time, a filter's cutoff. It is a control signal, not something anyone
hears directly. Every effect this week is built the same way: one LFO,
wired to a different parameter depending on the effect.
</p>

<div class="bench-annotated">
  <LfoWaveformDiagram targetLabel="Whatever the effect modulates" minLabel="minimum" maxLabel="maximum" />
  <p class="bench-prose">
  The two traces are the same waveform. The left one is the LFO itself; the
  right one is that same shape reframed as "the parameter this effect
  varies," swinging between whatever minimum and maximum that parameter
  takes. Only the framing changes from effect to effect.
  </p>
</div>

## Tremolo: modulating amplitude

<p class="bench-prose">
**Tremolo** is the simplest case: the LFO drives the signal's amplitude
directly, so the output rises and falls in loudness at the LFO's rate. No
delay, no filtering — just gain, moved continuously instead of held fixed.
</p>

## Vibrato: modulating pitch

<p class="bench-prose">
**Vibrato** is periodic *pitch* modulation rather than amplitude
modulation. The standard way to produce it electronically is to run the
signal through a very short delay line and modulate the delay time itself
with the LFO: continuously shortening and lengthening a delay stretches and
compresses the signal passing through it, which is heard as the pitch
rising and falling — a small, fast Doppler-like effect, not a literal
pitch-shifting algorithm.
</p>

</BenchBand>

## Chorus and flanger: two delayed copies, two different delay ranges

<p class="bench-prose">
Chorus and flanger both work by combining the dry signal with a copy of
itself passed through an LFO-modulated delay line — mixed back in, rather
than replacing the original the way vibrato does. What separates them is
simply how short that delay is.
</p>

<div class="bench-annotated">
  <DryWetPairDiagram wetLabel="Modulated delayed copy" />
  <p class="bench-prose">
  **Chorus** uses a delay long enough (tens of milliseconds) that the ear
  hears the copy as a second, subtly detuned voice alongside the original —
  the sound of two people meant to be in unison rather than one signal
  interacting with itself. **Flanger** uses much shorter delay times (a few
  milliseconds), short enough that the ear no longer hears "two voices" but
  instead a single, moving, metallic-sounding shift in timbre.
  </p>
</div>

## Why flanging sounds the way it does: a moving comb filter

<p class="bench-prose">
At flanger-length delays, summing the dry and delayed copies produces
**interference**: at some frequencies the two copies arrive in phase and
reinforce each other (constructive interference); at others they arrive out
of phase and cancel (destructive interference). Sweeping the delay time
sweeps *which* frequencies fall into each category, producing a **comb
filter** — a response with a regularly spaced series of notches — whose
notches visibly move as the LFO cycles.
</p>

<div class="bench-annotated">
  <NotchResponseDiagram kind="comb" />
  <p class="bench-prose">
  Each trace is the comb filter's response at one instant of the sweep; a
  shorter delay spaces the notches further apart. A static diagram can only
  show a few snapshots — in the live bench below, the notches actually sweep
  as the LFO cycles.
  </p>
</div>

## Phaser: interference without a delay line

<p class="bench-prose">
A **phaser** reaches the same kind of interference by a different route.
Instead of a delay line, the signal passes through a cascade of
**phase-shifting filter stages**, each of which shifts the phase of
different frequencies by different amounts without changing their level.
Summing that phase-shifted copy with the original produces the same
constructive/destructive interference as a comb filter — but the notch
positions are set by how much phase each frequency has picked up, not by a
travel-time delay. LFO-modulating the filter stages' corner frequency sweeps
those notches, exactly as a flanger sweeps its delay-based ones.
</p>

<div class="bench-annotated">
  <NotchResponseDiagram kind="phaser" />
  <p class="bench-prose">
  A cascade of a few phase-shifting stages typically produces only a
  handful of moving notches — visibly fewer and less evenly spaced than a
  flanger's comb filter, which is the usual audible way to tell the two
  effects apart even though both are built from the same underlying
  interference.
  </p>
</div>

## Try it: rate, depth, and mix, across five effects

<p class="bench-prose">
Switch between Tremolo, Vibrato, Chorus, Flanger, and Phaser. Rate and Depth
always mean the same thing — how fast and how far the LFO swings a
parameter — but watch the panel underneath the knobs: it shows exactly
which parameter is moving and between what values, which changes with the
effect even though the two knobs controlling it don't.
