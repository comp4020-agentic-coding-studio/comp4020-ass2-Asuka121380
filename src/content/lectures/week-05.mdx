<WeekBand index={1} label="Where we left off" heading="From linear filtering to nonlinear transformation">

Every stage covered so far — pickup, volume pot, tone control, cable — has
been **linear**: it can reshape which frequencies are present, but it never
adds anything that wasn't already there. Gain changes that. Once a signal is
amplified far enough, the system running out of room to represent it stops
being a detail and becomes the whole story. By the end of this week you
should be able to distinguish gain from output volume; explain linear
amplification; define headroom conceptually; explain clipping in terms of
input-output limits; distinguish hard and soft clipping; and explain why
clipping introduces harmonic content that wasn't in the original signal.

<WeekBand index={2} label="Gain versus volume" heading="Running out of headroom" layout="wide">

<GainStageDiagram />

**Gain** is amplification applied *within* a stage: for an ideal linear
amplifier, output(t) = G · input(t) — the waveform's shape is unchanged, only
its amplitude scales. **Volume** is a separate, later control over how loud
the already-processed signal ends up. A real stage can't raise amplitude
indefinitely, though: it has a maximum level it can represent before
something has to give. The gap between a signal's current level and that
maximum is its **headroom**. As gain increases, headroom shrinks — and once
it reaches zero, the waveform can no longer just get bigger.

</WeekBand>

<WeekBand index={3} label="Clipping" heading="What happens when headroom runs out">

**Clipping** is what a system does once it can't represent an amplified
waveform's true peak: rather than continuing to follow G · input(t), the
output stops tracking the input once it nears the limit. *How* it stops
tracking it is a design choice, not a single fixed behaviour — and that
choice is exactly what separates the two clipping families below.

</WeekBand>

<WeekBand index={4} label="Two clipping families" heading="Linear, soft, and hard transfer curves" layout="wide">

<CurveGallery>
  <TransferCurveCard
    label="Linear (no clipping)"

    curve={linearCurve}
  />
  <TransferCurveCard
    label="Soft clipping"
    description="Output bends away from the straight line gradually as it nears the limit — a smooth compression, not an abrupt wall."

  <TransferCurveCard
    label="Hard clipping"
    description="Output tracks the input exactly until an abrupt threshold, then is flattened outright — a sharp corner in the curve."
    curve={hardCurve}
  />

Either way, the output is no longer a scaled copy of the input — it's a
genuinely different waveform shape. A **nonlinear** transformation is exactly
one where output isn't simply proportional to input, and clipping is the
simplest example this course studies. A clipped sine wave contains frequency
components that were never present in the clean sine at all: flattening the
waveform's peaks is, in the frequency domain, equivalent to adding new
harmonics on top of the fundamental. No detailed derivation is needed here —
what matters is the causal link: *reshape the waveform → change its harmonic
content*. Those extra harmonics change the signal's perceived timbre, though
"more harmonics" on its own doesn't mean "better tone" — whether added
harmonics sound pleasant depends on which ones appear and how they're
balanced, not just how many there are.

</WeekBand>
