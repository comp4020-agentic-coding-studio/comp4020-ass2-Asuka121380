# Why does clipping sound different from circuit to circuit?

Same input. Same idea — limit the signal. Different results.

---

## Review: linear amplification

```text
y(t) = G · x(t)
```

Amplitude changes. Waveform shape does not.

---

## Review: clipping

Push a signal past a system's headroom and the output stops following
`G · x(t)`.

The waveform is reshaped — and a reshaped waveform has a different
spectrum.

---

## A simplified distortion pedal

```text
Input
  ↓
Gain stage
  ↓
Clipping stage
  ↓
Tone filter
  ↓
Output
```

Four stages. Today is about the middle two.

---

## The gain stage

An op-amp stage raises the signal past the level the next stage can pass
cleanly.

More gain here means the clipping stage has more to clip.

---

## Diodes as nonlinear elements

A diode conducts more readily in one direction than the other.

Used across a signal path, that asymmetry becomes a soft limit — a
conceptual model, not a full semiconductor account.

---

## Symmetric clipping

Positive and negative halves of the waveform are limited the same way.

The result stays close to odd-symmetric — a clue to which harmonics
appear next.

---

## Asymmetric clipping

Positive and negative halves are limited *differently*.

Breaking the symmetry changes which new harmonics appear, not just how
many.

---

## Feedback-loop clipping

Put the nonlinear element inside an amplifier's feedback path, rather
than directly across the signal.

Same limiting idea, different circuit context — and a different
transfer curve.

---

## Clipping to ground

Put the nonlinear element straight across the signal, shunting
excess swing to ground once it's reached.

Compare this transfer curve with the feedback case.

---

{/* _class: centered */}

## Comparing transfer curves

Four circuit ideas. Four input → output curves.

The curve *is* the circuit's clipping behaviour, stripped of everything
else.

---

## Comparing waveforms

Same input sine, four curves applied.

Symmetric curves keep the waveform's odd symmetry; asymmetric ones
don't.

---

## Comparing spectra

Every clipped waveform gains harmonics absent from the clean sine.

Which harmonics, and how strong, depends on which curve produced the
waveform.

---

## What makes fuzz different?

Fuzz circuits often push nonlinearity further than a single diode pair —
extra gain stages, transistor behaviour, more aggressive shaping.

Not one topology. A family of stronger, often less predictable,
nonlinear transformations.

## Main conclusions

- Clipping is nonlinearity; circuits differ in *where* and *how* they
  apply it.
- Symmetric vs. asymmetric clipping changes which harmonics appear.
- "Overdrive," "distortion" and "fuzz" name different *amounts and kinds*
  of nonlinearity, not points on one dial.
